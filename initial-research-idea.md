Differentiated International Integration in International Alliances
International alliances are commonly characterized at the alliance level as more or less integrated, internationally coordinated, or locally responsive. Such aggregate characterizations may conceal substantial variation within alliances. Exploratory interviews with managers involved in international joint ventures and other cross-border alliances suggest that international integration can differ markedly across functional areas and hierarchical levels. Senior management may operate through strongly cross-border structures while operational activities remain locally organized; technical functions may integrate personnel from both partners while HR, sales, or government-relations activities remain embedded in national or parent-company structures. These observations suggest that international integration is not necessarily a uniform attribute of an alliance but may instead take the form of an internally differentiated organizational configuration.
We develop the concept of differentiated international integration, defined as systematic within-alliance variation in the degree to which organizational domains combine personnel, knowledge, coordination, and influence across national and partner-firm boundaries. Building on the differentiated-network perspective and contingency arguments in international organization, we propose that such differentiation arises because organizational domains face different requirements for cross-border coordination and local embeddedness. Functions characterized by strong interdependence between alliance partners should exhibit higher cross-partartner integration, whereas functions whose activities depend strongly on local institutions, labor markets, customers, or regulatory relationships should exhibit greater localization. Hierarchical levels may similarly differ because senior managers are responsible for partner coordination and alliance-wide decision making whereas lower organizational levels may remain more closely tied to local operations.
We further argue that differentiated international integration is dynamic. Changes in the external environment do not necessarily make an alliance uniformly more or less international. Instead, environmental changes alter the relative coordination and local-embeddedness requirements facing different organizational domains and thereby trigger selective reconfiguration. A geopolitical or regulatory shock, for example, may increase the need for joint coordination at senior levels while simultaneously increasing localization in politically sensitive or operational functions. The resulting response is therefore a change in the configuration of integration rather than a uniform shift in alliance internationalization.
We investigate these arguments using an exploratory sequential mixed-method design. In a first qualitative phase, existing interviews with managers involved in international alliances are reanalyzed systematically to identify the dimensions through which respondents describe within-alliance differences in international integration. The qualitative analysis informs construct development and the operationalization of differentiated international integration. The second and primary empirical study examines a longitudinal sample of international joint ventures. IJVs provide identifiable organizational boundaries and therefore permit systematic observation of employees and organizational domains. IJVs are identified using Orbis Crossborder Investment, and employee histories are used to reconstruct the composition of individual functions and hierarchical levels over time.
The proposed quantitative unit of analysis is the IJV-function-year, supplemented where feasible by IJV-hierarchical-level-year observations. International integration is operationalized primarily through employees' prior organizational affiliations rather than nationality alone. For each organizational domain, we distinguish personnel originating from parent A, parent B, and the external or local labor market. Cross-partner integration captures the degree to which personnel from both parent organizations are represented within a domain; parent dominance captures concentration around one parent; and localization captures reliance on employees recruited independently from the parent organizations. We then examine whether environmental changes alter these configurations differently across organizational domains.
The study contributes to international business research in three ways. First, it shifts the conceptualization of alliance integration from an alliance-level attribute to an internally differentiated organizational configuration. Second, it extends differentiated-network reasoning beyond the boundaries of the multinational enterprise to interorganizational collaboration. Third, it develops a dynamic perspective in which environmental change produces selective reconfiguration across alliance domains rather than a uniform increase or decrease in international integration. The study thereby connects research on international alliance organization with broader questions concerning how internationally dispersed organizations adapt their internal configurations to changes in their external environment.

Quantitative Empirical Research Design
Differentiated International Integration in International Joint Ventures
1. Research Objective
The quantitative study tests whether international integration is systematically differentiated within international joint ventures (IJVs) and, more importantly, whether changes in the external environment selectively reconfigure integration across organizational domains. The empirical design therefore treats integration as a within-IJV configuration rather than as a single alliance-level characteristic.
2. Proposed Hypotheses
H1: The greater a functional domain's dependence on coordination and knowledge exchange between the IJV partners, the greater its level of cross-partner integration.
H2: The greater a functional domain's dependence on host-country institutional and market embeddedness, the greater its level of local staffing relative to cross-parent staffing.
H3: Cross-partner integration will be greater at higher hierarchical levels than at lower hierarchical levels within IJVs.
H4: Following deterioration in political relations between the parent firms' home countries, organizational domains with greater exposure to local institutional and political demands will become more localized.
H5: Following deterioration in political relations between the parent firms' home countries, hierarchical levels responsible for alliance-wide coordination will maintain or increase cross-partner integration relative to lower organizational levels.
H4 and H5 assume geopolitical deterioration as the focal environmental change. A different shock, such as investment-screening reform, mobility restrictions, or another regulatory change, could be substituted if it provides stronger empirical identification and adequate variation in the final sample.
3. Sample
The recommended population is international equity joint ventures rather than international alliances generally. IJVs provide identifiable organizational entities that can potentially be matched across Orbis and Revelio and permit observation of employees, functions, hierarchical levels, and personnel flows.
At least two identifiable corporate parents headquartered in different countries.
A separately identifiable JV legal entity and formation date.
A meaningful operating period, preferably at least three years.
Sufficient workforce coverage in Revelio; an initial screening threshold of approximately 20-30 observable employees is advisable.
No initial restriction to 50/50 ownership; ownership asymmetry should instead be measured and controlled.
A desirable target is approximately 300-800 usable IJVs observed over several years. Around 200 usable IJVs would be a practical lower target for a conventional longitudinal panel design, although a smaller sample could remain viable if the study exploits a sharply defined quasi-exogenous shock. Before committing to the full design, a pilot matching exercise should select approximately 100 known IJVs from Orbis and determine how many can be identified as distinct Revelio entities with adequate workforce histories.
4. Unit of Analysis
The primary unit of analysis should be IJV x function x year. A parallel dataset can be constructed at the IJV x hierarchical level x year. If employee histories permit sufficiently precise timing, quarterly or monthly observations could be considered for an event-study design, but annual observations are the safer starting point.
5. Data Sources
Orbis Crossborder Investment: identification of IJVs, parent firms, ownership shares, formation dates, locations, and transaction characteristics.
Orbis / Compustat / Capital IQ: parent- and JV-level financial and organizational controls where available.
Revelio Labs: employment histories, employer transitions, occupational/function classifications, seniority, location, and workforce composition.
BoardEx: supplementary information on senior executives and boards where coverage permits.
Country-dyad and policy/event datasets: measurement of geopolitical deterioration, sanctions, investment restrictions, or other environmental changes.
6. Core Dependent Variables
6.1 Cross-parent integration
For each IJV-function-year, identify employees whose previous organizational affiliation is Parent A, Parent B, or neither parent. Cross-parent integration should capture the extent to which both parent organizations are represented in the focal domain. A balance or entropy measure can be used. For two parent-origin categories, one possible measure is 1 - (ShareA^2 + ShareB^2), calculated among employees originating from the parents and rescaled if desired.
6.2 Parent dominance
Parent dominance captures whether one parent disproportionately supplies personnel to the focal organizational domain. A simple measure is |ShareA - ShareB|. Ownership shares should be incorporated because asymmetric equity stakes may legitimately generate asymmetric staffing.
6.3 Localization
Localization is the share of employees in the focal organizational domain who were recruited from outside either parent organization. A stronger version should distinguish employees recruited in the IJV host country from internationally recruited outsiders.
6.4 Within-IJV differentiation
Differentiation can be examined directly by retaining function-level and hierarchical-level observations. This is preferable to immediately collapsing the data into a single dispersion index. Robustness analyses could nevertheless calculate within-IJV dispersion in integration across functions or the difference between senior-level and operational-level integration.
7. Functional and Hierarchical Measures
Functions should be aggregated into a manageable number of broad categories to ensure adequate employee counts within cells. A starting classification could include R&D/engineering, operations/manufacturing, supply chain/procurement, finance, HR, sales/marketing, IT, and general management. The final categories should be determined by Revelio coverage and by the dimensions identified in the qualitative study.
The theoretical model should avoid relying solely on function dummy variables. Functions should, where possible, be characterized by underlying requirements for partner coordination and local embeddedness. The qualitative interviews can inform this classification. Hierarchical analysis can distinguish executives/senior management, middle management, professional employees, and operational employees, subject to Revelio's available seniority classifications and cell sizes.
8. Focal Environmental Change
The study should focus on one type of environmental change rather than combining unrelated shocks. Geopolitical deterioration between the parent firms' home countries is a promising candidate because it can simultaneously increase pressures for localization and the need for alliance-level coordination. Potential measures include discrete sanctions or diplomatic events, changes in bilateral political relations, investment restrictions, or validated country-dyad indicators. The final choice should be driven by theoretical fit, temporal precision, cross-dyad variation, and the possibility of credible identification.
9. Controls
JV age and size.
Ownership balance / equity shares.
Number and identity of parent firms.
Parent-firm size and performance.
Industry and host country.
Geographic and institutional distance between parent countries and the host country, where theoretically justified.
Host-country economic and institutional conditions.
Year effects and, where appropriate, JV fixed effects.
10. Estimation Strategy
The baseline analysis can estimate integration at the IJV-function-year level with JV and year fixed effects and interactions between environmental change and theoretically defined domain characteristics. The central empirical question is whether the same external change produces different integration responses across organizational domains.
If a sufficiently discrete and plausibly exogenous geopolitical or regulatory shock can be identified, an event-study or difference-in-differences design would be preferable. Treated IJVs would be those exposed through the relevant parent-country dyad, with comparable unexposed IJVs providing the control group. Pre-trend analysis would then be essential.
11. Role of the Qualitative Study in Measurement
The existing interviews should be systematically recoded before the quantitative measures are finalized. The reanalysis should establish (1) where respondents perceive differentiation - functions, hierarchy, decision rights, staffing, or partner affiliation; (2) what observable practices respondents associate with integration; and (3) why particular organizational domains require more cross-partner coordination or more local embeddedness. This step is important for establishing construct validity and avoiding an archival proxy that is too distant from the theoretical concept.
12. Recommended Feasibility Sequence
Recode the existing interviews specifically for differentiated international integration and its proposed mechanisms.
Draw approximately 100 international IJVs from Orbis Crossborder Investment.
Match the IJVs and their parents to Revelio and record match success, employee counts, functions, seniority, and observable parent-to-JV personnel transitions.
Assess whether parent-origin staffing can be reconstructed reliably and whether enough IJV-function-year cells meet minimum employee thresholds.
Only after this pilot, select the focal environmental shock and finalize the hypotheses and identification strategy.
13. Main Empirical Risk
The principal feasibility risk is entity matching and workforce coverage rather than the statistical model. The project depends on identifying enough IJVs as distinct Revelio entities and observing enough employees within each entity to distinguish functions, hierarchical levels, and parent-origin histories. The proposed 100-IJV pilot should therefore precede extensive hypothesis development or data construction.
