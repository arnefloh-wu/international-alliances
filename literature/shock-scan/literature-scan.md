## Cross-cutting picture

Reviews and agenda pieces now treat geopolitics as a mainstream IB topic:
Andrews, Puhr and Knill (2026, JIBP) synthesize 159 articles on how
geopolitical shocks are transmitted to firm behavior; Sinani, Hobdari, Brandl
and Gammelgaard (2026, IBR) call for work on corporate nationality,
organizational design and nonmarket capabilities; Moura et al. (2025, IJMR)
and Patsiaouras et al. (2026, MBR) review responses and risk. The common
outcome sets are location, entry mode, ownership, exit, investment, trade,
innovation and disclosure. Internal organization appears as an agenda item
(Sinani et al.) but rarely as a measured outcome, and nothing found measures
staffing by function or hierarchical level inside an IJV after a shock.

Closest to our design, in descending order of closeness:

1. Tang (2026, AOM Proceedings): bilateral political tension and innovation
   restructuring in Chinese MNEs, with headquarters and subsidiaries following
   different logics (within-firm unit differentiation, patents not staffing).
2. Tian, Xu and Yiu (2025, JoM): foreign director exit in China when
   bilateral relations deteriorate, 1,014 directors 1999 to 2018 (top-level
   staffing response; firm not alliance).
3. Liu (2026, JMS): voluntary turnover of foreign directors after sanctions on
   peer firms (top-level staffing; anticipation).
4. Lei et al. (2025, AOM Proceedings): Entity List sanctions lead Chinese MNEs
   to tighten control and prefer wholly owned subsidiaries over JVs (ownership,
   PSM-DID 2011 to 2023).
5. Sawant, Ameyaw, Treviño and Li (2026, JIBS): MNE responses to the
   Russia-Ukraine war; staying firms transfer resources to the subsidiary and
   downgrade non-location-bound advantages (qualitative; resource
   reconfiguration).
6. Zhang (2024, RIPE): trade-war political risk raised firm exits from China
   by 34 percent, and JV status raised exit probability.
7. Glennon (Management Science): H-1B restrictions raised US multinationals'
   foreign-affiliate employment (staffing location, not composition).
8. Zhou, Li and Wang (2024, JIBS): bilateral tension and local patenting by
   foreign subsidiaries in China.

None of these observes within-alliance configurations by function or level.
The gap claimed in the research idea is not contradicted by anything found,
but the manual S9 runs are needed before saying so in prose.

## Family A. Sanctions and secondary sanctions

Heavily studied: trade and welfare effects of sanctions (Crozet and Hinz on
Russia 2014; Crozet et al. on exporters); Western divestment from Russia after
2022 (Evenett and Pisani 2023 estimate 5 to 13 percent divestment in the first
nine months; Ahmed, Davydov and Solanko 2026 compare stayers and leavers);
conceptual reviews of IB under sanctions (Meyer, Fang, Panibratov, Peng and
Gaur 2023); whether sanctions work (Gaur et al. 2023); the stay-or-exit
dilemma (JWB 2024).

Underexplored: what happens inside foreign units and alliances that stay;
which domains are localized and which keep parent staff; top-level versus
operational responses. Sawant et al. (2026) and Liu (2026) are the nearest.

Closeness: moderate for S02 (Russia 2022) on resource reconfiguration; low on
staffing composition.

## Family B. Investment screening and national security review

Heavily studied: the diffusion and design of screening mechanisms in
political science and law (Bauerle Danzman and Meunier 2023, PRISM; Alami
2024; EU-focused work); effects on cross-border M&A flows (Eichenauer and Wang
2024: an 11.7 to 16.0 percent fall in M&A in newly screened sectors, driven by
minority, state-linked and US-investor deals); effects on venture capital
(Kiel WP 2282; a survey of Chinese investors on FIRRMA in the Illinois Law
Review).

Underexplored: effects on existing alliances and their internal staffing.
Screening operates mainly at deal entry; mitigation agreements that restrict
a foreign parent's access to technology or data would be the channel to
staffing, but they are confidential and no study of their organizational
consequences was found.

Closeness: low. Novelty for S06 is high, but so is the risk that the
treatment barely touches existing IJVs.

## Family C. Export controls and technology restrictions

Heavily studied (economics, mostly since 2022): supply-chain decoupling after
US export controls (Crosignani, Han, Macchiavelli and Silva 2024: US
suppliers end relations with Chinese customers, lose market value and cut
employment); innovation effects of Entity List restrictions on Chinese firms
and their US collaborators (CEPR column; Humanities and Social Sciences
Communications 2024 on R&D intensity); the Japan-Korea 2019 controls
(Makioka and Zhang 2024: trade fell for hydrogen fluoride; Japanese affiliates'
local production in Korea rose).

Underexplored: organization of alliances that straddle a listed and a
non-listed parent; ring-fencing of R&D and IT personnel; compliance roles.
Lei et al. (2025) on ownership is the only management study found that uses
the Entity List as a treatment for firm organization.

Closeness: moderate for S09 (Lei et al. use the same treatment and a DiD on
ownership); none on staffing composition.

## Family D. Mobility, visa and work-permit restrictions

Heavily studied: high-skilled immigration policy and firm location
(Glennon: H-1B restrictions shift employment to foreign affiliates in Canada,
India and China); US-China science tensions (Jia et al. 2024, PNAS, on the
NIH investigations; Aghion et al. on the China Initiative); visa regimes
(Czaika et al. 2018); Brexit effects on trade, services exports and
relocation (JIBP 2025 on services exports), and on migration (Economics
Letters 2025 on the end of free movement).

Underexplored: how mobility restrictions change who staffs which domain and
level inside cross-border units. A JIBS 2025 piece revisiting global-mobility
research under "hyperglobalization at a crossroads" frames this as an agenda.
No study of IJV staffing by function or level after Brexit was found.

Closeness: low to moderate (Glennon is closest in design logic for S14/S17;
nothing close for S13).

## Family E. Bilateral political deterioration

Heavily studied: effects of bilateral relations on FDI location, entry and
ownership (Li, Meyer, Zhang and Ding 2018; public sentiment and acquisition
ownership, JIBS 2022; geopolitical alignment and greenfield FDI); market
reactions to Sino-Japanese shocks (Fisman, Hamao and Wang 2014: effects
smaller for firms with high Chinese employment); trade punishment for Dalai
Lama meetings (Fuchs and Klann); volatility of political affinity and
subsidiary investment including employees (Adarkwah et al. 2024, SMJ);
foreign director exit (Tian et al. 2025); local patenting as a signal (Zhou
et al. 2024); exit under trade-war risk (Zhang 2024); resilience of US
subsidiaries in China (JIM 2025).

Underexplored: discrete coercion episodes as staggered treatments for
alliance organization; which domains of a foreign-partner IJV localize under
consumer and regulatory pressure. Older qualitative work notes that
political tension made Japanese firms reluctant to localize management in
China (ResearchGate item, authors not seen), which runs opposite to the
localization direction and is a useful warning that predictions by level are
not obvious.

Closeness: moderate for S19 (top-level staffing in Tian et al.; within-MNE
differentiation in Tang 2026).

## Family F. Data localization and data-transfer rules

Heavily studied: costs and trade effects of data localization in policy and
economics work (ICRIER 2020; agent-based modeling in Telecommunications
Policy; think-tank estimates of hosting-cost increases). Legal analysis of
China's CSL and PIPL and of Schrems II is abundant.

Underexplored: effects on the organization of cross-border units. No IB study
of how data rules change which personnel staff IT, HR or analytics functions
in foreign units or alliances was found. A management study using LinkedIn
data would face the irony documented in S23: Russia's localization law led to
LinkedIn's block.

Closeness: none found. Novelty high; theoretical leverage is mostly one
family (regulatory exposure of data-intensive functions).

## Family G. Ownership caps, governance rules and forced divestment

Heavily studied: China's JV requirement in autos and knowledge spillovers
(Bai, Barwick, Cao and Li, published in the American Economic Review per the
snippet), with flows of employees from JVs to affiliated domestic firms as a
main conduit; legal analyses of the Foreign Investment Law conversion (Chow).

Underexplored: within-JV staffing when ownership caps or governance rules
change. India's 2021 insurance reform, which moved CEO and board appointment
rights, is a level-specific contingency change with no organizational study
found.

Closeness: Bai et al. use employee flows in Chinese auto JVs, close to our
measurement logic but not to our outcome.

## Family H. Tariffs and trade wars

Heavily studied: incidence and trade effects of the 2018 to 2019 US-China
tariffs (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein; Fajgelbaum et al., cited in the
snippet but not linked here) and a growing literature on 2025 tariffs.
Organizational and staffing effects inside alliances are not studied, and the
contingency logic gives no clear differential prediction for staffing.
